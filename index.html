Na cúig thuisealThe five cases, and which ones actually change a word

Irish lists five cases. Only three change the shape of a noun: nominative (the baseline), genitive, vocative. The accusative and the dative are jobs, not forms.

TuisealCaseIts jobDoes the noun change?
AinmneachNominativeSubject; dictionary formBaseline
CuspóireachAccusativeDirect object of a conjugated verbNo — identical to nominative
TabharthachDativeAfter a simple prepositionNo — mutation only, plus relics
GinideachGenitivePossession, "of", after verbal nouns and compound prepositionsYes — the real work
GairmeachVocativeDirect addressYes — lenite, and slenderise

An chodarsnacht is tábhachtaí — the contrast that earns most marks

Chuala sé an chaintconjugated verb → object is accusative = nominative form
Bhí sé ag éisteacht leis an chaintverbal noun + simple preposition → dative
Bhí sé ag déanamh na cainteverbal noun + direct object → genitive

An chaint in the first line is lenited because caint is feminine and follows an — not because of any case ending. Mutation and case are two different systems that happen to meet at the article.

Cé acu tuiseal? — a decision procedure

  1. Is the noun being spoken to? → gairmeach. a Sheáin
  2. Does an "of" or an apostrophe-s fit in the English? → ginideach. doras an tí
  3. Is it the object of a verbal noun? → ginideach. ag ól an bhainne
  4. Does a simple preposition come first? → tabharthach (mutation only). ar an bhord
  5. Anything else — subject or object of a conjugated verb → plain nominative form.

Na díochlaontaThe five declensions — how the genitive singular is built

DíochlaonadhTypical nounRuleSamplaí
1úmasculine, ends in a broad consonant Caolaigh — slenderise the ending bád → báid · fear → fir · sagart → sagairt · bealach → bealaigh
2úfeminine, ends in a consonant Caolaigh + -e cos → coise · bróg → bróige · -each → cailleach → caillí
3úagent nouns in -óir/-eoir/-úir; abstracts in -acht; mixed Leathnaigh + -a múinteoir → múinteora · bliain → bliana · rang → ranga · filíocht → filíochta
4úends in a vowel, or in -ín Gan athrú — no change ever baile → baile · cailín → cailín · oíche → oíche
5úmostly feminine; ends in a vowel or -ir Leathnaigh + -ach / -n cathair → cathrach · athair → athar · cara → carad · Éire → Éireann

Trí sheift — three shortcuts

Vowel-final is free. If a noun ends in a vowel or -ín, it is 4th declension and never changes: an bhaile, an chailín, na hoíche. The 5th-declension nouns that also end in a vowel are a short closed list (cara, caora, lacha, comharsa, Éire, teanga*) — learn those six and the rule holds.

-óir / -eoir / -úir / -acht is always 3rd. Broaden and add -a. This covers every job title and every abstract noun you will ever need: an dochtúra, an fheirmeora, na filíochta.

Masculine and broad? Try slenderising first. The 1st declension is the largest class. If an fear → an fhir feels right by ear, it usually is.

* teanga is in fact 4th — no change. It sits in the list only because it looks like the others.

Iolra lag agus iolra láidir — weak and strong plurals

The genitive plural depends on which kind of plural the noun has.

CineálTestGenitive pluralSampla
Lag (weak)Plural made by slenderising or by adding -aReverts to the nominative singular form, eclipsed after nana fir → obair na bhfear
Láidir (strong)Plural ends in -í, -tha, -anna, -acha, -ta…Identical to the nominative pluralna ranganna → obair na ranganna

This is why rang matters: it is 3rd declension with a strong genitive plural — na ranganna, unchanged.

An GinideachWhen it fires, and the traps around it

Na spreagthaí — the triggers

  1. Noun + noun. doras an tí · carr an fhir
  2. After a verbal noun with a definite object. ag ól an bhainne · ag déanamh na hoibre
  3. After a compound preposition. tar éis, i ndiaidh, in aghaidh, in éadan, ar feadh, os cionn, os comhair, ar son, ar fud, i lár, de bharr, in aice
  4. After certain simple prepositions. chun, trasna, timpeall, cois, dála — chun na scoile
  5. After quantity words. neart, go leor, cuid, roinnt, a lán — mo chuid airgid, neart oibre

An dá eisceacht a bhaineann tuisle asat — the two trip-wires

1. in aice le takes the dative, not the genitive. The compound preposition proper is in aice + genitive (in aice na scoile). Add le and you are on a simple preposition, so the noun reverts: in aice leis an scoil. Same shape as os comhair an tí versus os mo chomhair.

2. A verbal-noun object that is indefinite and carries an adjective stays nominative. ag ceannach carr nua — not cairr nuaidh. No article, adjective attached, genitive collapses.

Riail an ghinidigh fhada — the genitive chain

One definite article per chain, and it goes on the last noun. Every noun upstream drops its article and lenites where it can.

Trí sheift a chlúdaíonn beagnach gach cás

Draw the X. Feminine lenites in the nominative singular; masculine lenites in the genitive singular. The two genders swap behaviour between the two cases. One diagonal each.

na + plural + genitive = urú, always. No exceptions, no gender split: obair na bhfear, praghas na n-úll, teach na mban.

sc-, sp-, sm-, st- block the ts-. an scoil, an spéir, an stail — never an tscoil. The cluster cannot be pronounced, so the language never asks for it.

DNTLS — the letters that refuse séimhiú after an

d and t are never lenited after an: an doras → an dorais, ar an teach. s takes ts- instead of séimhiú. l, n, r have no written séimhiú at all, so nothing shows.

Say it as one word to fix it: an-doras, an-teach. The tongue is already in position for the d or t, so it will not soften.

An TabharthachThe dative, and where Ulster splits from the rest

Modern Irish has almost no distinct dative form left. What survives is the mutation after preposition + article — and this is the clearest dialect fault line in the whole grammar.

An riail Ultach

UlaidhCaighdeán / Connachta / An Mhumhain
on the tablear an bhordar an mbord
with the teacherleis an mhúinteoirleis an múinteoir
at the manag an fhearag an bhfear
in the housesa bhailesa bhaile

Ulster lenites; the south eclipses. The 2017 Caighdeán now permits the Ulster lenition as a variant, so both are defensible — but be consistent inside one piece of writing.

Na heisceachtaí

ar an doras · ar an teach — d and t are immune.
ar an tsráid · ag an tsagart — s becomes ts.
ar an scoil — s-clusters take nothing at all.
ag na fir — plural na triggers no mutation whatsoever.

Iarsmaí an tsean-tabharthaigh — the surviving old datives

A handful of feminine nouns kept a real dative form, and they live on in fixed phrases:

in Éirinn · i nDoire · ar an tsráid · ó bhaile · le láimh · ar muin, ar bord, i gcionn

in Éirinn is the one that has to be automatic — it is the dative of Éire (genitive na hÉireann), and all three forms appear constantly.

Íomhá le cuimhneamh air

The vocative a is a hand reaching out to pull the word towards you: it softens (séimhiú) and it sharpens (caolú). Everything the vocative does follows from that one gesture.

Botúin choitianta

The a is never dropped in writing, even though speech swallows it. Dia duit, a Sheáin — not Dia duit, Sheáin.

Names beginning with a vowel or with L, N, R show nothing: a Aoife, a Liam, a Nóra. Nothing visible does not mean nothing happened — the particle is still required.

Struchtúr na hAbairteWord order, and where each case sits in it

Irish is VSO: Briathar – Ainmní – Cuspóir, then adverbials in the order Modh, Áit, Am — manner, place, time.

Cheannaigh an fear an carr go tapa sa bhaile inné.

V — S — O — manner — place — time. English stacks them the same way, so trust your ear on this one, unlike everywhere else.
